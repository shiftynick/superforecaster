Brier score — how forecasts are graded

The single number that decides every architecture race in this project.

After a question resolves, take your probability, take what actually happened (1 if it happened, 0 if not), and square the gap:

Brier = (your probability − outcome)²  —  lower is better, 0 is perfect.

You saidIt happened?ScoreVerdict
90%Yes(1 − 0.9)² = 0.01Excellent
90%No(0 − 0.9)² = 0.81Punished hard for confident wrongness
50%Either0.25The "always shrug" baseline
70%Yes0.09Good

Scores get averaged over many questions. The reference points to keep in mind: always answering 50% scores 0.25; a good human crowd scores around 0.15–0.19; the best published AI systems live in the 0.10–0.20 range depending on question difficulty. The gaps between good designs are tiny — hundredths — which is why we need hundreds of questions to tell two architectures apart, and why intuition can't judge them.

The squaring is what makes this an honesty machine: saying 95% when you should say 70% feels bolder, but across many questions it bleeds score. The best long-term strategy is to report exactly what you believe — which is why Brier is the standard for measuring forecasting skill.

Calibration & confidence

The property you said is extremely important — here's how it's measured.

Calibration means: of all the times you say "70%", the thing happens about 70% of the time. It's checked by bucketing past forecasts and comparing each bucket's stated probability to its actual hit rate:

This is separate from confidence in a specific forecast — how much signal the system actually found for this question (was there good data? did the passes agree?). Per your feedback, every forecast the system produces will carry both: the probability and an explicit confidence level. Low confidence never hides — in personal use it's flagged; in tournament play (where skipping costs points) the system falls back to submitting the community's number rather than a low-signal guess.

Tournament play — what it is and why we're in it

The phrase from the abstention discussion, unpacked.

Metaculus is a forecasting platform: it publishes resolvable questions ("Will X happen by date Y?"), thousands of people submit probabilities, questions resolve, and everyone's accumulated accuracy is scored. A tournament is a scored season of such questions. The AI Benchmark tournament is the bots-only division: you register a bot, it must forecast every question in the season via API, and its scores are compared against other bots and against Metaculus Pro forecasters (paid human superforecasters — currently still beating every bot).

Why we bother: it's the only external, un-gameable scorecard. Our backtests could fool us (data leaks, cherry-picking); a live tournament can't — the answers don't exist yet. The tournament is mode 3 of the system, existing to validate the engine that modes 1 and 2 (your decision support and reports) run on.

One consequence for abstention: tournament rules score every question, so "declining to forecast" means taking a default score — usually bad. That's why the design says: in tournament mode, when confidence is low, submit the community/market prior (a respectable neutral answer) instead of abstaining. In your personal modes there's no such constraint — the honest answer "I don't have enough signal" is allowed and valuable.

Combining forecasts — why not just average?

The math layer you asked to understand as it goes. Here's the intuition, no formulas required.

Suppose two forecasting passes come back with 95% and 50%.

  • Plain averaging says (95 + 50) / 2 = 72.5%. But this systematically drags confident answers toward the mushy middle — averaged forecasts are provably underconfident.
  • Log-odds pooling converts each probability to odds first (95% = 19-to-1; 50% = 1-to-1), averages in that space, and converts back: about 81%. The confident view keeps more of its weight.

The intuition: probability is a squashed scale — the difference between 95% and 99% is huge evidence-wise (5× less doubt), while 48% vs 52% is nearly nothing. Odds-space respects that; percent-space doesn't. On real tournament data (1,300+ forecasters), odds-space pooling reliably beats plain averaging. So the engine always combines forecasts in log-odds space — one of the few free wins the literature offers.

Two refinements stack on top, both applied after pooling — see next section.

Extremization & Platt scaling — the two dials after pooling

Both are single-number corrections learned from past performance.

Extremization — push the pooled answer further from 50%… sometimes

When several forecasters each saw different private evidence and all lean the same way, the truth is usually more extreme than their average — each was hedging for what they didn't know, but collectively they knew it. So classic aggregation extremizes: pooled 75% becomes, say, 85%.

The catch that matters for us: this only works when the forecasters' information is independent. Our ensemble members are LLM passes sharing the same training data and much of the same retrieval — mostly shared information. The evidence says: with shared information, extremize mildly or not at all (sometimes even soften). So our extremization dial starts near "off" and is tuned on backtest data, not copied from the human-crowd literature.

Platt scaling — fix the model's systematic bias

LLMs habitually hedge toward 50% (trained to be cautious). Platt scaling is a tiny correction curve fitted on past resolved questions: "when this system says 65%, reality behaves like 74% — so stretch its outputs accordingly." It's the last step in the pipeline and one of the confirmed techniques in the current best system. Think of it as calibrating the instrument after manufacturing.

A — Checklist single-passbaseline

One careful pass, following the human superforecaster's checklist. Everything else is measured against this.

The "checklist" is the distilled human craft: anchor on how often things like this generally happen (the outside view), then adjust for what's specific here (the inside view), then actively try to prove yourself wrong. Published work shows this single-pass recipe already gets near human-crowd level.

Role in the race: it's the baseline and the first live tournament entry — cheapest to run (~$0.17/question at Sonnet), simplest to debug, and every other candidate must beat it to earn its complexity.

B — Diverse ensemblestrong evidence

Many deliberately-different passes, combined with the math from the primers above.

The diversity is engineered, not accidental: different reasoning orders, different personas, different retrieval, even different underlying models. The spread of the answers is itself the confidence signal — five passes landing on 15–30% is a very different situation from five landing on 20–22%.

Your "competence weighting" instinct lives here too: passes that historically score better get more weight in the pool (the mechanized version of "tracking", the best-evidenced human intervention).

Evidence: a 12-model ensemble already matched a 925-person human crowd in a live tournament. This is the literature's predicted winner on pure accuracy. ~$1.40/question at Sonnet.

C — Decomposition pyramidyour idea — open hypothesis

Your founding intuition, drawn as you described it: break the question down to bedrock, bubble confidence back up.

Confirming your description back to you: yes — what you described is exactly this. Hierarchical breakdown of the primary question into prerequisite questions, recursively, down to a bedrock of researchable facts and base rates; then confidence and probability scores from the bottom of the pyramid combine upward to produce the final number. In the literature this family is called Fermi decomposition / Bayesian-network forecasting. Your version's distinctive feature is the per-node confidence riding alongside each probability.

Where the evidence stands: genuinely open. Our research pass specifically hunted for studies testing whether this improves or hurts calibration — and found no confirmed evidence either way. The theoretical worry is error compounding (bottom of the diagram); the design guards are depth caps, an outside-view anchor at every node, and never trusting a long multiplied chain without a holistic cross-check. Our backtest will be among the first direct tests.

Three ways it can win, even if it loses on raw Brier: (1) outright accuracy; (2) as the explanation layer — this diagram is a legible argument for the number, which no other candidate naturally produces; (3) as a diversity generator feeding ensemble B (a decomposition-based pass thinks differently from a holistic pass).

D — Market as a weighted inputconfirmed component

Reframed per your feedback: the market price is another data point — potentially heavily weighted, never the sole reliance.

Markets aggregate thousands of self-interested forecasters, which makes the price a genuinely strong signal — the best published system loses to the market head-to-head but wins when blended with it, meaning the AI adds real information the market missed. Your framing is exactly how we'll build it: the price enters the pipeline as one more input with a learned weight. If backtests say weight it heavily, we weight it heavily; on questions with no market (many of the ones you'll care about personally), the engine stands alone — which is why D is a component, not the whole system.

E — Supervisor-reconciled scaffold2025 state of the art

Ensemble B, but the combining formula is replaced by a reasoning agent that reads the arguments.

The difference from B is subtle but important: B combines the numbers with a formula; E has a strong model read the reasoning behind each number and judge — spotting double-counted evidence, noticing when one pass found something the others missed. That's something no formula can do. It's the architecture of the current best published system (Bridgewater's AIA Forecaster, which also pioneered the Platt-calibration step).

Trade-off: the most expensive per question (every ensemble cost plus a long-context supervisor call — roughly $2–3/question with an Opus supervisor). We race it after B, and only if B's formula looks like the bottleneck.
