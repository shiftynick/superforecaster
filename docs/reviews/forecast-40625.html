When an appellate court grants an injunction pending appeal, it typically signals that the plaintiffs are likely to succeed on the merits. The base rate for an enjoined law being fully reinstated in its original form by a later date is low, perhaps 15-25%. 3. SPECIFICS ADJUSTING THE BASE RATE: - On November 18, 2025, the Ninth Circuit granted an injunction staying SB 261 enforcement pending appeal, while allowing SB 253 to proceed [S7, S8, S16, S27, S30]. This selective injunction signals the panel sees stronger First Amendment problems with SB 261 specifically. - Oral argument on January 9, 2026 reinforced skepticism: the panel pressed California on how SB 261's broad narrative disclosures could avoid being political and noted the disclosures were 'ill-defined' [S9]. Judge Nguyen asked pointed questions about SB 261's breadth [S9, S10]. - For YES, multiple conditions must all hold: (a) the Ninth Circuit must issue a merits decision before September 1, 2026 (~7.5 months after oral argument, plausible at ~70-75%); (b) the panel must rule in favor of California on SB 261, dissolving the injunction (~15-20% given the signals); (c) no Supreme Court stay intervenes (~80% chance none does); (d) California does not amend the law (~90% chance no amendment). - Multiplying: 0.72 * 0.18 * 0.80 * 0.90 ≈ 0.094, roughly 9-10%. - The fine print requiring the ORIGINAL law (not rewritten) adds another hurdle: if the court identifies specific problematic provisions and California amends the law, that would not qualify. 4. STRONGEST COUNTERARGUMENT: The panel did not uniformly side with plaintiffs. Additionally, the commercial speech doctrine has historically been forgiving of disclosure requirements, and a panel with two Republican appointees might still uphold the law under Zauderer's lighter standard. If the panel rules relatively quickly (by spring 2026) and upholds SB 261 under commercial speech doctrine, the path to YES opens. This could push the estimate up to perhaps 15%. 5. FINAL: Balancing the signals, the injunction plus skeptical oral argument heavily favor plaintiffs on SB 261. The narrow path to YES requires a timely pro-California ruling with no intervening stays or amendments.
